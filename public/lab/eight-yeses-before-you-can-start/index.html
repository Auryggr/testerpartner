Eight yeses before you can start

An online renewal that asks you to be sure of eight things at once. And the checkout version of the same problem: rules your customers discover too late.

The story

Daniel finally has a free evening to renew his passport, and he's glad it can be done online. Before he can start, the page asks him to be sure of eight things at once: the passport's original validity, its expiration window, his age, that his name hasn't changed, that he isn't traveling soon, that he's in the country, that the passport is with him and undamaged, and that he doesn't need it fast.E2

He has a trip in five weeks. He reads one line three times.

The friction

  • All-or-nothing eligibility. You can renew online only if you meet all eight conditions; otherwise you're sent to renew by mail or in person.E2E3
  • One rule that can be read two ways. "Not traveling for at least 6 weeks from the date you will submit your application." Does it mean no trip starting in the next six weeks, or no trip lasting six weeks?E8
  • Jargon. "Online renewals cannot be expedited", "mutilated" and "(or was) valid for 10 years" appear without plain-language explanations.E2
  • A clock you can't see. If you come back later, your session may expire and you'll have to start over.E7
  • Time lives somewhere else. Fees are on the page; processing time is on another one.E4E5

To be fair, the page also does something many sites don't: it warns people to avoid unofficial renewal sites and names the only official one.E6

Hypotheses

  1. Asking the eight conditions one at a time, as a short yes/no checker before the application, would reduce the number of people who start, fail a condition halfway, and leave.E2E3
  2. Rewriting the travel rule in plain language ("Don't apply online if your trip starts in the next 6 weeks") would reduce wrong self-assessments and support calls.E8
  3. Letting people save and resume instead of starting over would increase completed applications among people who need to fetch a photo or a document.E7

How we'd validate it without an A/B test

  • A comprehension test with 5 to 8 people: show the travel rule and ask what it means. If the answers split, hypothesis 2 is confirmed before writing a line of code.
  • Funnel analysis: where people drop between "apply now" and submission, and how many sessions time out.
  • Review support-call topics about eligibility and expired sessions.
The same friction in your ecommerce

The rules your customers find at checkout

The store version: a shopper reaches checkout and only then learns the product doesn't ship to their region, the promo code doesn't apply to sale items, or the subscription has a minimum term. Or they step away to find their card and come back to an empty cart.

Surfacing restrictions on the product page and keeping carts alive are among the first experiments we'd consider when checkout abandonment is high.
