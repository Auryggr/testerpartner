The price on the door

A passport renewal page that answers who it's for, what it costs and what can go wrong before you click anything. And the question it leaves for another page.

The story

Maya has a wedding abroad in two months and a passport that expires next spring. She types the question everyone types: how do I renew my passport?

On the first page she opens, before she has clicked anything, she already knows three things: who the service is for, what it costs, and that booking flights before the new passport arrives is a bad idea.E1E2E3E5

The friction (and the lack of it)

This page is a benchmark because it answers the questions that usually make people leave:

  • Who is it for? You must be aged 16 or over, or turning 16 in the next 3 weeks. Children are sent to a separate process.E3
  • How much? The online price and the paper-form price, side by side. The cheaper digital route is visible from the start.E2
  • What could go wrong? A warning not to book travel until you hold a valid passport, because the new one comes with a different number.E5
  • Where am I? Three short sections: overview, renew, and replace a lost, stolen or damaged passport.E7

The one question it doesn't answer on the page is how long it will take. Maya has to follow "Check how long it will take" to another page.E4

Hypotheses

  1. Showing a typical processing time on the overview would reduce visits to the separate service-information page and the uncertainty that pushes people toward paid urgent options.E4
  2. Stating the price difference as a saving ("save £13.50 by applying online") would move more applicants to the digital route than listing both prices.E2
  3. A clear "Start now" action on the overview would reduce hesitation compared with a pagination-style "Next: Renew" link.E6

How we'd validate it without an A/B test

  • Count how often people move from the overview to the processing-time page, and what they do next.
  • Run a five-second test: show the overview and ask "How long will this take?" and "What's the next step?".
  • Review contact-centre topics for "how long" questions before and after adding the time.
The same friction in your ecommerce

Your product page has a "how long?" too

This is a product page done right: price, who it's for and the main risk, all above the fold. Most stores get the price right and then hide the answer to the next question, when will it arrive?, behind a shipping-policy link or until checkout.

Putting a delivery estimate on the product page, and framing your cheaper option as a saving, are two of the first hypotheses we'd put on the table.
